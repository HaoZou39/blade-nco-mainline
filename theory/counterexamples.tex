\section{Adversarial finite-state checks}
The examples below are exact toy models. They test universal statements and
assumptions; none is asserted to be the BLADE-NCO implementation.
Their tags report mathematical status; implementation applicability is
\istatus{NOT APPLICABLE as a universal guarantee}, and novelty is
\nstatus{NOT CLAIMED}.

\subsection{Stale memory after a target shift}
\label{sec:counterexamples}
\mstatus{DISPROVED} for the claim that historical memory cannot hurt.
Take $\mathcal{X}=\{a,b\}$. Before a change the hard region is
$\HARD_0=\{a\}$; just before the first measured proposal it shifts to
$\HARD_i=\{b\}$ for all $i\geq1$. The inherited population contains only
$a$. The memory policy mutates from $a$ to $a$ with probability $0.99$ and to
$b$ with probability $0.01$, retaining $a$ after a miss. Each proposal costs
one unit. Its hitting time is geometric with success probability $0.01$,
so $\Ex[\tau_M]=100$ and
\[
 \Prb(\tau_M\leq B)=1-0.99^B.
\]
A fixed generator that independently samples $a$ or $b$ uniformly every
proposal has $\Ex[\tau_G]=2$ and success probability $1-0.5^B$. Thus the
memory policy is strictly worse at every positive integer budget. The target
has undergone a single non-stationary shift; the failure is stale structural
support, not an independence artifact.

\subsection{Gradient adaptation can beat structural mutation}
\mstatus{DISPROVED} for any universal claim that structural mutation
outperforms HAC-style gradient adaptation. Let
$\mathcal{X}=\{0,1,\ldots,L\}$ and $\HARD=\{L\}$. A structural mutation
from $i<L$ advances to $i+1$ with probability $\varepsilon$ and otherwise
stays at $i$. Starting at $0$, its expected number of unit-cost proposals to
hit $L$ is $L/\varepsilon$. A gradient-informed comparator that advances
deterministically one step per unit-cost proposal hits in $L$ proposals.
For any $0<\varepsilon<1$, the structural policy is slower. This is a toy
landscape with an informative gradient, not a model of the actual HAC
implementation; it proves that operator labels alone imply no ranking.

\subsection{Diversity can allocate effort away from the target}
\mstatus{DISPROVED} for the claim that increased diversity always improves
first hitting time. Let $\mathcal{X}=\{s,u,g\}$, target $\HARD=\{g\}$,
and one proposal cost one unit. The diverse parent set contains distinct
niches $s$ and $u$; parent selection is uniform. Mutation from $s$ reaches
$g$ surely, whereas mutation from $u$ stays at $u$. Hence the per-proposal
hit probability is $1/2$ and expected hitting cost is $2$. A focused policy
that selects $s$ every time hits in cost $1$. Preserving the irrelevant
niche does not help this target and reduces access to the useful parent.

\subsection{Perfect current-hardness prediction need not preserve a bridge}
\mstatus{DISPROVED} for the claim that perfect prediction of current BQ
hardness makes score-elite retention preserve future mutation reachability.
Consider states $\{a,b,g\}$ and current target $\HARD=\{g\}$; neither $a$
nor $b$ is itself a hit, and their current true hardness is equal:
$h(a)=h(b)<\kappa$. From $a$, the next scheduled mutation reaches $g$
surely; from $b$, mutation stays at $b$ surely. Let the surrogate be perfect
on current hardness, so $\widehat h(a)=h(a)=h(b)=\widehat h(b)$. A one-slot
score-elite update must still break the tie. If its deterministic tie rule
retains $b$, it discards $a$ and makes $g$ unreachable. Thus current-hardness
accuracy, even perfection, does not identify equal-hardness candidates with
different mutation futures. A reachability-aware rule could retain $a$, but
perfect current-hardness prediction alone need not do so. This is not a claim
that the observed S6-AE hash tie-break selected such a pair; it is an exact
obstruction to the proposed bridge.

\subsection{Monotone niche occupancy with progressive bridge loss}
\label{sec:progressive-bridge-loss}
\mstatus{PROVED counterexample} to the claim that monotone niche occupancy
preserves future hard-region reachability. Fix population capacity $m$ and an
integer $2\leq L\leq\min\{K-1,m-1\}$. Use occupied niches
$0,1,\ldots,L$. Initially $P_0$ contains a bridge $a_i$ in niche $i$ for
each $1\leq i\leq L$, together with $m-L$ distinct fillers
$f_1,\ldots,f_{m-L}$ in niche $0$.

At cycle $i-1$, one designated operator applied to $a_i$ produces a valid
decoy $d_i$ in the same niche $i$ with probability one. Every other scheduled
attempt returns the rejection symbol $\partial$; no attempt is resampled. Set
the current true hardness and a perfect current predictor so that
\[
 h(a_i)=\widehat h(a_i)=1,\qquad
 h(d_i)=\widehat h(d_i)=2,\qquad
 h(f_j)=\widehat h(f_j)=3,
\]
with deterministic distinct hash order where needed. Choose the current hard
threshold above $3$, so none of these candidates is a current hit.

In niche $i$, $d_i$ replaces $a_i$ as the score elite. Niche $0$ contributes
one filler to the elite floor, and the remaining $m-L-1$ fillers occupy all
global-fill slots. Thus $a_i$ fails both tests in
Lemma~\ref{lem:membership} and is discarded. All as-yet-unprocessed $a_j$
remain the sole candidates in their niches and therefore survive. Inductively,
after cycle $t$ the selected population still occupies exactly
$\{0,1,\ldots,L\}$, while the number of retained bridges has fallen from $L$
to $L-t$.

After cycle $L-1$, let the future true-hard region be $\{g\}$. A future
designated operator reaches $g$ surely from any $a_i$ and cannot reach it from
any $d_i$ or $f_j$. The selected population now contains no $a_i$, so its
future hit probability is zero, even though its niche occupancy never
decreased. A population retaining any discarded $a_i$ would hit surely. The
failure is not surrogate error: the predictor is perfect for current hardness.
It is a mismatch between current-hardness quality and future-reachability
value.

This construction includes the complete elite floor and global score fill,
not merely a one-slot archive. Taking, for example, $m=10{,}000$ and
$L\leq999$ respects the S6-AE capacity ratio. It is a formal counterexample,
not evidence that these states or score relations occurred in S6-AE.

\subsection{Niche aggregation can destroy the Markov property}
\mstatus{DISPROVED} for the assertion that a niche label alone is a Markov
state. Let full states $x_1,x_2$ both map to niche $A$, while $z,w$ map to
distinct niches $Z,W$. From $x_1$, the next state is $z$ with probability
$1$; from $x_2$, the next state is $w$ with probability $1$. Both full states
have the same current aggregate $A$, but the next aggregate is respectively
$Z$ or $W$. The transition probability out of aggregate $A$ is therefore
not determined by the aggregate alone (strong lumpability fails). Therefore
niche aggregation is not Markov uniformly over initial laws. For a specially
chosen initial distribution, an averaged transition might nevertheless be
Markov; that weaker distribution-dependent claim would require the
conditional within-$A$ mixture to remain history-independent. The example
does not disprove every such weak aggregation. A proof using only niche counts
is invalid unless it proves the relevant strong or distribution-dependent
condition, or augments the state with the hidden structural/solver
information.

\subsection{Hard-instance presence is not novel discovery}
\mstatus{PROVED definitional counterexample} to treating the parent-inclusive
generation hit as new-instance discovery. Let $P_k=\{x\}$,
$h_k^{\rm BQ}(x)\geq\kappa$, and all scheduled child attempts be rejected,
so $O_k=\varnothing$ and $C_k=\{x\}$. Then $D_k^{\rm gen}$ holds, while
$D_k^{\rm new,gen}$ is false whenever $x$ was previously generated.
For a separate cycle-0 edge case, suppose $P_0=\{x\}$ and a
valid, accepted mutation returns exactly $x$, so $O_0=\{x\}$ before
parent--child union deduplication. Although
$\mathcal V_{-1}^{\rm gen}=\varnothing$, this offspring is not a novel
phenotype because $x\in P_0$. The corrected event
$D_0^{\rm new,gen}$ therefore excludes it. After every structural niche
is already occupied, a later offspring can still be a new, distinct hard
phenotype within an old niche. Therefore newly occupied niches, new hard
phenotypes, acquired novelty, and hardness reactivation must not be
conflated.
